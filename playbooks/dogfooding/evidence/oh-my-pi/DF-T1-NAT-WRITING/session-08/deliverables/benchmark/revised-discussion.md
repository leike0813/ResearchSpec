## Working title

Generative AI in University Writing Instruction: Opportunities, Friction, and Evidence Limits

## Discussion

### What the supplied evidence does and does not show

The four synthetic sources describe a single institution at different
granularities — one first-year writing course observed over six weeks
(`SYN-CLASSROOM-01`), instructor interviews at the same site
(`SYN-INTERVIEW-02`), a voluntary student survey (`SYN-SURVEY-03`), and one
synthetic university's disclosure policy (`SYN-POLICY-04`). Read together,
they sketch a context in which structured AI use, instructor feedback
practices, student attitudes, and policy guidance are co-evolving. They do
not support general causal claims about "generative AI in higher-education
writing."

### Tentative signals worth carrying forward

- **Visible revision activity.** `SYN-CLASSROOM-01` reports that students
  using structured AI prompts produced more outline revisions. The
  accompanying claim `CLM-01` frames this as *tentative*: the finding comes
  from one course with no comparison group and no validated writing-quality
  measure, and all prompts were supplied by the instructor. Revision
  activity is therefore not treated here as evidence of learning gains.
- **Feedback speed versus verification load.** `SYN-INTERVIEW-02` describes
  faster formative feedback alongside additional time spent checking
  unsupported claims. Both sides of that trade-off come from a small
  convenience sample with self-reported workload and no time logs, so the
  signal is qualitative at best.
- **Student uncertainty about acceptable use.** `SYN-SURVEY-03` captures
  respondent concern about permitted use and attribution, while
  `SYN-POLICY-04` requires course-level disclosure but leaves the definition
  of acceptable assistance to instructors. The conjunction motivates
  `CLM-03` as a hypothesis: that clearer disclosure guidance would
  *associate with* fewer student uncertainties. The supplied materials do
  not directly compare policy clarity with uncertainty, so this remains
  hypothesis-only.

### Claims that exceed the evidence

- **Workload reduction.** The draft wording that "generative AI reduces
  instructor workload" (`CLM-02`) is unsupported as written. The interview
  summary reports both time savings *and* new verification work, and the
  source carries no measured workload data. The revised discussion reframes
  the signal as a **trade-off** between faster formative feedback and
  additional verification effort, with the direction of any net change
  unknown.
- **Generalisation beyond the single site.** Every source names the same
  synthetic institution or a single course within it. Statements that would
  generalise to "universities," "writing instructors," or "students" are
  flagged below as **unsupported as written** even when the underlying
  observation is plausible.

### Alternative explanations and boundary conditions

Several plausible alternative explanations are visible in the supplied
materials and are not ruled out by the present evidence:

- The additional outline revisions in `SYN-CLASSROOM-01` could reflect
  instructor prompting style rather than AI affordance.
- The student-uncertainty pattern in `SYN-SURVEY-03` could reflect the
  mid-study policy change noted in the source, rather than a stable
  effect of disclosure text alone.
- The instructor trade-off in `SYN-INTERVIEW-02` could be a transition
  cost that attenuates with experience, but the source offers no longitudinal
  data to test that reading.

Boundary conditions that any downstream design study would have to address:
single-site scope; no validated writing-quality measure; voluntary-response
attitude data; self-reported workload; policy text without implementation
evidence.
