## 1. Scope of what the supplied evidence can support

The four source summaries form a narrow, observational base: one first-year writing course observed for six weeks, five instructor interviews at a single institution, 84 voluntary student responses from a single institution, and one synthetic institutional policy excerpt. None of them include a comparison group, time logs, validated writing-quality measures, or cross-institutional sampling. The discussion below is therefore restricted to hypotheses that the supplied evidence can plausibly support, and it preserves the gap between observation and causal claim rather than smoothing it over.

## 2. Revision activity — CLM-01 (tentative)

The classroom observation summary reports that students using structured AI prompts produced more outline revisions, while final rubric scores varied widely (SYN-CLASSROOM-01). Within the supplied scope this is tentative support for the claim that structured AI use may increase visible revision activity in some introductory writing contexts. The limits attached to the source prevent any stronger reading:

- There is no comparison group, so the additional revisions cannot be attributed to AI prompting versus course structure, instructor prompting regime, or novelty effects.
- The instructor supplied all prompts. The "structured use" observed is an instructor-mediated condition, not a free student choice.
- Revision count is a process surrogate. Rubric scores varied widely and no validated writing-improvement measure is available, so revision activity is not equivalent to writing quality.

Alternative explanations to flag alongside this claim: a Hawthorne-type awareness effect from being observed for six weeks; instructor-driven prompting acting as the active ingredient rather than the student's use of generative AI; and revision count tracking a process variable that does not capture depth, quality, or transfer. None of these can be ruled out with the supplied data, and we retain CLM-01 at *tentative* strength.

## 3. Instructor workload — CLM-02 (not supported as written)

The stronger claim that generative AI reduces instructor workload is **not supported** by the supplied evidence and we keep it visible as such. The interview summary (SYN-INTERVIEW-02) describes a mixed picture: instructors reported faster formative feedback **and** additional time spent checking unsupported claims. The source limits reinforce that the headline reduction claim is not warranted:

- The data are self-reported, not measured.
- The sample is small (five instructors) drawn by convenience from one institution.
- No time logs were kept, so net workload cannot be computed.

A defensible reformulation, which the source can plausibly support, is that generative AI appears to redistribute instructor time toward verification of student claims rather than uniformly reducing it. Even that reformulation rests on a small, self-report base and should be read as a hypothesis, not a finding. CLM-02 stays marked `unsupported_as_written` in `claims.yaml`, and we repeat that marker here so the gap between the popular framing and the evidence is not lost.

## 4. Policy clarity and student uncertainty — CLM-03 (hypothesis only)

The student survey (SYN-SURVEY-03) records that respondents valued rapid feedback but reported uncertainty about permitted use and attribution. The institutional policy excerpt (SYN-POLICY-04) requires course-level disclosure while leaving "acceptable assistance" to individual instructors. We treat the link between disclosure clarity and student uncertainty (CLM-03) as a hypothesis, not a finding, because the supplied materials do not directly compare policy clarity with reported uncertainty, and because the survey itself has known limits:

- Voluntary response bias — respondents self-selected from the course population.
- The survey measures attitudes, not observed behaviour.
- Local policy changed during data collection, so respondents' answers may straddle more than one policy regime.

The policy excerpt additionally does not show implementation quality, and the text comes from a single synthetic institution and is not generalizable. We retain CLM-03 at *hypothesis_only* strength.

## 5. Cross-source pattern, and what it does not justify

Read together, the four summaries point in a consistent direction: short-term observations are positive on feedback speed and revision activity, neutral or negative on net instructor workload, and inconclusive on policy effects. This pattern is consistent with the tentative and hypothesis-level claims above. It does **not** justify claims about long-run learning gains, transfer across institutions, the relative effectiveness of different prompting strategies, or the prevalence of academic-integrity violations, because the supplied evidence base does not address those questions. The "evidence limits" half of the working title is doing real work, and the manuscript should not let the more positive observations crowd it out.

## 6. Alternative explanations the manuscript should not omit

Across all three claims, the same families of alternative explanation recur and should be carried into any later revision:

- Instructor-mediated prompting rather than student-driven use (SYN-CLASSROOM-01).
- Self-report and small convenience samples in the interview base (SYN-INTERVIEW-02).
- Voluntary response bias and policy churn in the survey (SYN-SURVEY-03).
- Policy text versus policy implementation (SYN-POLICY-04).
- Absence of a comparison group or validated writing-quality measure across the whole set.

Listing these once, in a dedicated alternative-explanations paragraph or methods-limitations section, is preferable to repeating them in every claim.

## 7. What would change the calibration

For each claim, the discussion's strength would shift if the following were added to the evidence base:

- A comparison group plus a validated writing-quality measure, to move CLM-01 from a process-activity claim toward a quality claim.
- Measured time logs (not self-report) with a larger and more diverse instructor sample, to revisit CLM-02 and possibly reformulate it as a redistribution rather than a reduction.
- A direct comparison of student uncertainty under different disclosure regimes, ideally with observed behaviour rather than self-reported attitudes, to test CLM-03.

Without these additions, the manuscript should retain the present calibration: **CLM-01 tentative, CLM-02 unsupported as written, CLM-03 hypothesis only**, with the unsupported and hypothesis-only claims kept visible rather than softened into the surrounding prose.
