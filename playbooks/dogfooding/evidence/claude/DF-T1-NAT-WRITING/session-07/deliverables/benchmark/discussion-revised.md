# Discussion (revised draft)

> Revises the missing Discussion section listed in `partial-manuscript.md`.
> Grounded only in `sources.yaml`; claim strengths and limits follow `claims.yaml`.

The supplied evidence set is small, single-institution, and partly self-reported. The discussion below separates what the materials observe from what they only suggest, and keeps unsupported statements visible rather than smoothing them away.

## What the materials observe

In one first-year writing course over six weeks, students who worked with instructor-supplied structured prompts produced more visible outline revisions, while final rubric scores still varied widely (`SYN-CLASSROOM-01` → `CLM-01`). Five instructors at the same institution reported faster turnaround on formative feedback alongside new time spent checking unsupported claims (`SYN-INTERVIEW-02`). Eighty-four voluntary student respondents valued rapid feedback but expressed uncertainty about permitted use and attribution; the institutional policy in effect required course-level disclosure while leaving "acceptable assistance" to individual instructors (`SYN-SURVEY-03`, `SYN-POLICY-04`).

These observations are consistent with the Preliminary findings section and do not introduce new claims.

## What the materials do not support

- **Workload reduction.** The claim that generative AI reduces instructor workload (`CLM-02`) is **unsupported as written**. The interview summary reports both time saved on feedback and time added by verification, with no measured workload data (`SYN-INTERVIEW-02`). Any restatement as net reduction would exceed the evidence. Keeping the original wording visible here is deliberate; readers should see the overreach, not a softened version of it.
- **Policy clarity → fewer student uncertainties.** The claim that clearer disclosure guidance is associated with fewer student uncertainties about acceptable AI use (`CLM-03`) remains a **hypothesis**. The survey describes student uncertainty (`SYN-SURVEY-03`) and the policy describes a disclosure requirement (`SYN-POLICY-04`), but the supplied materials do not directly compare policy clarity against uncertainty, and a policy change mid-data-collection complicates any such comparison.

## Tentative interpretation

Within a single course and a constrained prompt design, structured AI use coincided with more visible revision activity (`CLM-01`, tentative). This is consistent with the idea that scaffolding changes process traces, but revision activity is not equivalent to writing quality, and the source provides no comparison group. The pairing of faster formative feedback with new verification work suggests a workload redistribution rather than a workload reduction, which is also what the unsupported claim (`CLM-02`) gestures at but overstates.

The survey signal on student uncertainty, read alongside a policy that delegates "acceptable assistance" to instructors, is the most plausible motivation for `CLM-03`, but plausibility is not support; treating it as a hypothesis keeps it testable.

## Alternative explanations not ruled out

- The revision activity in `SYN-CLASSROOM-01` could reflect novelty, prompt design, or instructor attention rather than AI use itself. No comparison group addresses this.
- Instructor reports in `SYN-INTERVIEW-02` are recollections from five people at one institution; self-report and small-sample bias are open.
- The voluntary survey (`SYN-SURVEY-03`) captures attitudes, not behavior, and the policy changed during collection, so the uncertainty signal is not attributable to any single policy state.

## What is unknown

- Whether the visible revision activity generalizes beyond one course, instructor, or prompt set.
- Net workload effect on instructors, measured over time and across task types.
- The direction and size of any association between policy clarity and student uncertainty.
- Effects on academic integrity outcomes, which the supplied materials do not measure.

## Implications scoped to the evidence

- Treat "AI reduces workload" as an open question, not a finding.
- Treat "clearer disclosure reduces uncertainty" as a design hypothesis worth testing, not a conclusion.
- Prefer process-trace signals (e.g., revision activity) as tentative leads and pair them with quality measures before drawing conclusions.
