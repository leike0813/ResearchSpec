## Evidence scope

Four synthetic sources sit behind this discussion: a six-week classroom observation in one first-year writing course, an interview summary from five instructors at one institution, an 84-response voluntary student survey, and one institutional policy excerpt. The sources do not share an outcome scale, and none includes a pre-registered comparison. Anything beyond a narrow, tentative reading of those four documents is out of scope.

## What the classroom observation supports, and what it does not

SYN-CLASSROOM-01 reports that students using structured AI prompts produced more outline revisions, while final rubric scores varied widely. That observation supports CLM-01 as written: structured prompting coincided with more visible revision activity in one introductory course. It does not support broader claims about writing quality, transfer to other courses, or durability of the effect, because the source flags no comparison group, no validated measure of writing improvement, and an instructor who supplied all the prompts.

## Workload: the unsupported claim, kept visible

CLM-02 as worded ("generative AI reduces instructor workload") is not supported by the supplied evidence. SYN-INTERVIEW-02 describes faster formative feedback alongside additional time spent checking unsupported claims, and it does so with self-reported impressions, no time logs, and a five-instructor convenience sample at one institution. The honest reading is that workload appears to shift, with feedback time down and verification time up; the net direction and magnitude are unknown. The manuscript should not state that AI reduces workload.

## Disclosure guidance and student uncertainty: a hypothesis, not a finding

CLM-03 ("clear disclosure guidance is associated with fewer student uncertainties about acceptable AI use") is also not directly supported by the supplied materials. SYN-SURVEY-03 records that respondents valued rapid feedback and that some reported uncertainty about permitted use and attribution. SYN-POLICY-04 requires course-level disclosure rules while leaving acceptable assistance to individual instructors. The sources document the existence of disclosure rules and the existence of student uncertainty; they do not connect policy clarity to uncertainty levels. The relationship is a hypothesis worth designing a study around, not a finding.

## Policy variation

The policy excerpt is a single institution. Acceptable assistance is delegated to instructors, which is itself a design choice with consequences for consistency across courses. Any inference about disclosure practices or instructor discretion is local to that one synthetic institution, and cross-institutional variation is outside the supplied materials.

## Alternative explanations

Several alternative explanations fit the classroom observation as well as the one the manuscript currently favours. The visible revisions could reflect the instructor-supplied prompts, instructor presence, novelty of the tool, or rubric criteria that rewarded revision traces, rather than the structure of the prompting itself. The interview summaries could reflect selection bias toward instructors willing to talk about AI. The survey could reflect voluntary response bias toward students with strong opinions about AI in writing. None of these alternatives can be ruled out from the four sources.

## Calibrated conclusion

Within the four supplied sources, one tentative pattern survives: structured AI prompting coincided with more visible revision activity in one introductory course (CLM-01, tentative). Two claims should not be presented as findings: that generative AI reduces instructor workload (CLM-02, unsupported as written) and that clear disclosure guidance reduces student uncertainty (CLM-03, hypothesis only). The evidence base is too thin for general causal statements about generative AI in university writing instruction.
